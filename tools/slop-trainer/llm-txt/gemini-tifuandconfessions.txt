I hid a speaker inside our walls three months ago to convince my wife our house is haunted, but now she’s calling an exorcist and I’ve gone too far.

It started innocently enough. We bought our first house last year, an old 1920s craftsman with a lot of "character" (creaky floors, weird drafts). My wife is obsessed with true crime and paranormal podcasts, and she’s always joked that she wanted a ghost. So, during a weekend home renovation project where I had a drywall panel removed in the hallway closet, I got a stupid idea. I tucked a tiny, Bluetooth-enabled magnetic speaker deep inside the wall cavity, patched it back up, and painted over it.

At first, I kept it subtle. Once a week while she was reading in the living room, I’d use an app on my phone to play a faint, three-second scratching sound. Then, maybe a tiny, muffled floorboard creak. She loved it. She’d look up, eyes wide, and whisper, "Did you hear that?" I’d play dumb and tell her it was just the house settling.

But then I got greedy. I started downloading high-quality sound effect libraries. I played a very faint, raspy sigh at 2:00 AM. I played the sound of a marble rolling across a ceiling. Two weeks ago, while she was home alone, I played a audio clip of a little girl whispering "hello" from inside the hallway wall.

That was the breaking point. She didn't find it fun anymore. She completely lost it.

She called me at work sobbing, saying the house is cursed. When I got home, she had placed lines of salt across every single doorway and window sill. She spent $400 on sage, crystals, and holy water, completely drenching our bedroom. But it gets worse. Yesterday, I came home to find a literal priest sitting at our kitchen table drinking coffee. She has officially hired a local paranormal investigative team and a priest to perform a full-blown house blessing and cleansing next weekend.

# The investigators want to drill into the drywall to check for "structural anomalies or hidden spaces where energy traps form." They are literally going to drill directly into the spot where my $30 Amazon speaker is hidden. If I confess now, she will realize I’ve been psychologically torturing her for ninety days, and she will probably divorce me. If I don't confess, a priest is going to throw holy water at my Bluetooth speaker while a guy with an EMF meter discovers a lithium-ion battery in the wall.

I accidentally convinced my entire corporate office that I am a world-class, classically trained pastry chef, and now I am responsible for catering the CEO’s retirement party next week.

I work in a mid-level data entry role at a logistics firm. I am completely invisible most of the time. Two months ago, it was my turn to bring snacks for the Friday morning team meeting. I completely forgot about it until 11:00 PM Thursday night. In a panic, I drove to a high-end, independent French bakery two towns over, bought two dozen luxury, artisanal croissants and tarts, took them home, scraped them out of the fancy boxes, and arranged them neatly on a cheap plastic platter from Walmart.

When I brought them in, my manager took a bite of a raspberry pistachio galette, closed his eyes, and asked where I bought them. For some stupid, impulsive, insecure reason, the words "Oh, I just whipped them up last night" flew out of my mouth.

I thought that would be the end of it. It was not.

The department head tasted one and practically wept. Within an hour, word spread across the three floors of our office building. People were stopping by my cubicle asking me about my "lamination process" and what percentage of European butter I use. I just nodded, smirked, and muttered things I looked up on Wikipedia like, "It's all about the hydration levels of the dough, really."

The lie snowballed into a monster. Last month was the regional VP's birthday. They asked if I could "do my magic" again. I had to wake up at 5:00 AM, drive back to the secret French bakery, spend $120 of my own money, and transfer the pastries into Tupperware containers dusted with flour so it looked authentic. I became an office celebrity.

Yesterday, the administrative assistant to the global CEO called me into the executive suite. The CEO is retiring after 35 years, and they want the event to feel "intimate and personal." She told me the company is allocating a $1,500 budget for me to personally bake the entire dessert spread for 80 high-profile executives, investors, and board members.

# They ordered a three-tier custom gateau Saint-Honoré and fifty individual chocolate soufflés. I don't even own a stand mixer. My apartment oven burns frozen pizzas if I leave them in for twelve minutes. I can’t buy them from the French bakery this time because the CEO’s assistant explicitly said she wants to come over to my place on Tuesday evening to film a "cute behind-the-scenes video of the office chef at work" for the company’s internal newsletter. I am completely ruined.

I have been paying a professional teenager to play a competitive online video game on my account for the last six months just so I can pretend to be a "cool gamer dad" to my teenage stepson.

When I married my wife last year, I became a stepfather to her 14-year-old son, "Leo." Leo is a good kid, but he is incredibly quiet and completely obsessed with a highly competitive first-person tactical shooter game. For the first few months of our marriage, our conversations were completely nonexistent. He just stayed in his room, and I had no idea how to connect with him.

In a desperate bid to find common ground, I downloaded the game. I tried playing it for a week, but my 42-year-old reflexes are completely dead. I was constantly eliminated within five seconds of spawning, and my teammates would regularly yell insults at me over the voice chat. When I tried to talk to Leo about the game, he just smirked and said, "Yeah, it’s a pretty high skill ceiling, it’s probably not your thing."

The rejection stung. So, I went on a freelance outsourcing website and found a 17-year-old kid from another state who is ranked in the top 1% of players globally. I offered to pay him $50 a week to log into my account every evening and absolutely dominate matches, slowly leveling my character up to the highest competitive tier.

The plan worked flawlessly. One evening, Leo looked up my profile to see if I was still struggling, and his jaw hit the floor. He saw my immaculate win-loss ratio and my incredibly rare top-tier rank badges. Suddenly, I wasn't an uncool stepdad anymore; I was a gaming prodigy who just "prefers to play casually without a mic."

He started asking me for strategic advice. I would text the 17-year-old kid for notes, memorize the terminology, and repeat it to Leo at breakfast. "Yeah, Leo, you really need to work on your crosshair placement and economy management on round three." Leo looked at me like I was a god. Our relationship has never been better; we hang out all the time now talking about esports.

# But the house of cards is falling apart. For my birthday next weekend, Leo used his own savings to buy us two tickets to a massive local LAN gaming tournament event. He entered us into the casual 2v2 duo bracket as a team. We are scheduled to play live on stage on a big screen in front of a live crowd of two hundred people. I don't even know what keys layout do what on the keyboard.

I accidentally started a full-blown neighborhood turf war over a plastic flamingo, and now our local Homeowners Association (HOA) is threatened with a lawsuit.

I live in a suburban neighborhood with an incredibly strict, overbearing HOA. The president of the board, "Brenda," is a nightmare. She walks around with a literal clipboard measuring grass height and issuing $50 fines if your garbage cans are visible from the street for ten minutes past noon.

Two months ago, my direct neighbor and best friend, "Dave," got hit with a citation for having a single, pink plastic lawn flamingo hidden behind his rose bushes. Brenda deemed it a "visual nuisance that degrades property values." Dave was annoyed, so he gave the flamingo to me. That night, after dark, I snuck over into Dave’s yard and planted the flamingo directly back in his garden bed.

The next day, Dave saw it, laughed, and thought I was messing with him. So, he waited until nightfall, snuck onto my property, and put the flamingo right in front of my porch. This became a hilarious, ongoing game of stealth midnight flamingo placement between us.

But then, other neighbors noticed. A couple down the street thought it was a silent protest against Brenda’s tyranny. They went out and bought six more plastic flamingos. Within three weeks, a secret underground movement formed. We created an anonymous neighborhood group chat called "The Pink Rebellion."

Every single night, a rotating group of twelve households would sneak out at 3:00 AM and completely relocate an army of now fifty flamingos onto different lawns. You’d wake up, look out your window, and find twenty pink birds staring at you. It was amazing. The neighborhood solidarity was beautiful.

Brenda, however, went into an absolute psychological meltdown. She viewed this as an organized insurrection against her authority. She began patrolling the neighborhood at night in her golf cart with a flashlight, trying to catch the "culprits." Last week, she unilaterally passed an emergency HOA amendment stating that anyone caught possessing or moving a plastic flamingo would face an immediate $500 fine and property lien threat.

Two nights ago, someone managed to superglue thirty flamingos directly onto the roof of Brenda’s house while she was asleep. She called the actual police department to report a "coordinated hate crime against property management." The police told her it was a civil matter and hung up. Now, she is spending thousands of dollars of our collective HOA community funds to install high-definition facial recognition security cameras on the street utility poles to catch us. Half the neighborhood is now hiring a lawyer to sue the board for misappropriation of funds. All because Dave didn't want to throw away a piece of plastic.
